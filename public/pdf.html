Historical Context

Purpose

This section provides the historical and art-historical framework for readers encountering al-Andalus and Madinat al-Zahrāʾ for the first time, while still offering depth for specialists through citations and footnotes. It situates the capital within Umayyad caliphal ambition, architectural rhetoric, and the material culture of medieval Iberia.

The Umayyad Caliphate in al-Andalus

2a — The Umayyad Caliphate in al-Andalus

▸ Background on the Umayyad dynasty’s arrival in Iberia (711 CE) and the establishment of the emirate, then caliphate, at Córdoba.
▸ ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III (r. 912–961): proclamation of the caliphate in 929; Córdoba as the most sophisticated city in western Europe.
▸ Competition with the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad and the Fatimid caliphate in North Africa — architecture as political rhetoric.
▸ Key source: Calvo Capilla, S. (2018), ‘The Visual Construction of the Umayyad Caliphate in Al-Andalus through the Great Mosque of Cordoba.’

Madinat al-Zahra - The Brilliant City

2b — Madinat al-Zahrāʾ - The Brilliant City

▸ Founded 936 CE on the slopes of the Sierra Morena above Córdoba — three terraces of palaces, mosques, gardens.
▸ Name meaning: ‘most radiant city’ or ‘shining city’ — architectural manifesto of caliphal legitimacy.
▸ Key spaces: the Salón Rico (Rich Hall / Throne Hall of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III), administrative quarters, mosque, gardens.
▸ Destruction: the fitna of 1009–1010 — civil war, sacking, and the city’s disappearance under farmland for ~1,000 years.
▸ Today: UNESCO World Heritage Site; active archaeological excavation; on-site museum.
▸ Key source: Vallejo Triano, A. (2015), ‘Madinat al-Zahrāʾ: Historical Reality and Present-Day Heritage.’

The Architecture of Capitals - Rhetoric in Stone

2c — The Architecture of Capitals - Rhetoric in Stone

▸ The Corinthian capital as a Mediterranean form reactivated and transformed by Umayyad craftsmen.
▸ Caliphal capitals: dense acanthus foliage, corner volutes, interlaced vegetal and geometric motifs — distinct from Roman precedents.
▸ The reuse of Roman spolia: thousands of antique columns imported from Ifriqiya (Tunisia/Algeria) and Tripolitania (Libya) — hybrid materiality.
▸ The inscription as architectural element: Kufic blessings on the abacus — invocations of divine authority woven into structure.
▸ Scholarly debate: Cressier, P. (2010) on the role of the capital in caliphal architectural discourse; Cressier, P. (1995) on the Rich Hall capitals.s

Formal description

3a — Formal Description

▸ Describe the capital’s physical form: deeply carved acanthus leaves on the drum, four corner volutes, interlaced vegetal and geometric motifs, string of gem-shaped motifs along the upper drum.
▸ Describe the abacus: Kufic inscriptions on all four faces — angular Arabic script carrying Quranic blessing phrases.
▸ Material: white marble — likely local Spanish quarry, though the column shaft it once stood on may have been imported spolia.
▸ Condition notes: some damage to inscription legibility (noted in Prof. Alibhai’s translation report).

The Kufic Inscription (feature content)

3b — The Kufic Inscription (feature content)

This is a major scholarly contribution of the project. Prof. Ali Asgar Alibhai (UT Dallas) has produced a complete reading and translation of the inscription. Present this as an annotated, sequential walkthrough of all nine inscription segments.
▸ Complete translation: ‘In the name of God the Merciful. Blessed is He who, if he willed, would make for you God benevolence. Blessings from God. Complete Happiness.’ (Alibhai, 2024)
▸ Transliteration: Bism Allāh al-Raḥīm, Tabāraka alladhi ʼin shāʼa jaʻala laka khayr Allāh. Baraka min Allāh. Ghibṭa Kāmila
▸ Note that some letters are no longer legible due to damage — Alibhai’s report specifies which segments are partially reconstructed.
▸ Contextualize the blessing phrases: ghibṭa kāmila (‘complete happiness’) is a well-attested ornamental phrase appearing on Andalusi ivories, metalwork, and carved ivories such as pyxides.

Roman Spolia and the Question of Recarving

3c — Roman Spolia and the Question of Recarving

▸ One research question: Was this capital originally a Roman Corinthian capital that was recarved by Umayyad craftsmen, or is it a wholly new creation?
▸ Evidence for recarving: proportional analysis; structural anomalies visible in 3D scan; comparison with known reused Roman capitals at Madinat al-Zahrāʾ.
▸ Significance: The reuse of Roman spolia was a deliberate caliphal strategy — appropriating the visual authority of Rome while asserting Islamic sovereignty. See Calvo Capilla (2014), ‘The Reuse of Classical Antiquity in the Palace of Madīnat al-Zahrāʾ.’
▸ Comparison slider: Before/After view — current state of the capital vs. a digital reconstruction of what the Roman prototype may have looked like. See comparison slider implementation notes below.

3D Scanning

3D Scanning!

Why Digital Methods?

4a — Why Digital Methods?

▸ Traditional photography cannot capture the full three-dimensionality of a carved capital — a 3D model allows 360° examination of carving depth, undercutting, and surface texture.
▸ The digital scan creates a permanent, non-contact record — a resource for conservation, future scholars, and institutions without physical access to the object.
▸ Foundation for a larger database of dispersed Madinat al-Zahrāʾ capitals worldwide (see Section 5).

The 3D Scanning Process

4b — The 3D Scanning Process

▸ Technology: EinScan HX2 hybrid structured-light / laser scanner — produces high-resolution mesh models.
▸ Process: The capital was scanned at the Meadows Museum during the Unearthing the Legacy of Islamic Spain exhibition. The scanning team included JT Ringer (DIG Lab Manager, SMU), Anna Shovlin (MA student, UT Dallas), and Nida Jaffer (MA student, UT Dallas). Multiple scan passes from different angles were aligned and merged to capture the deeply undercut surfaces. A complementary Matterport scan of the gallery space was also captured by Anna Shovlin to provide spatial and exhibitionary context.
▸ Output: Full OBJ mesh model + texture maps. Team is working to produce an optimized smaller OBJ for web/VR deployment.
▸ Challenges: The deeply undercut acanthus leaves and the capital’s multi-directional surfaces required careful repositioning to capture occluded areas.

What the Scan Reveals

4c — What the Scan Reveals

▸ Tool marks and carving sequence: the 3D model makes visible the order in which different elements were carved — offering clues to workshop practice.
▸ Structural evidence for the recarving hypothesis: the model allows measurement and comparison of proportional systems that suggest a Roman Corinthian prototype.
▸ Inscription legibility: the scan captures inscription segments that are difficult to read with the naked eye under flat lighting — raking light views generated from the 3D model enhance legibility. In addition, Dr. Luis Rueda Galán (Meadows Museum, SMU) conducted a hands-on examination of the capital using UV light, which revealed surface details and possible traces of material treatments not visible under standard lighting conditions. UV examination results should be cross-referenced with the 3D model in the final publication.

3D Printing and Public Engagement

4d — 3D Printing and Public Engagement

▸ A 3D-printed replica of the capital has been produced in multiple versions, enabling hands-on engagement in the museum gallery and educational settings. Initial prototypes were printed using fused deposition modeling (FDM) printers with 1.75mm ABS filament at SMU’s Deason Innovation Gym (DIG) and UT Dallas’s 3D printing lab. The capital was printed in five interlocking segments assembled with Velcro for easy disassembly. Higher-fidelity reproductions were subsequently produced using the 3D Systems ZPrinter 450, which builds forms from a gypsum-based composite powder bound by a starch-based inkjet binder; after printing, the fragile pieces are treated with ZBond (cyanoacrylate) to increase strength and produce a smooth, slightly glossy surface resembling marble. These prints were produced in collaboration with Dr. Matthew Boulanger, Archeology Research Collections (ARC), SMU.
▸ Tactile access: The printed replica allows visitors, including those with visual impairments, to experience the capital’s form through touch — a significant accessibility dimension.
▸ Classroom use: The replica and 3D model are being integrated into art history and archaeology courses at SMU and UT Dallas.

Virtual Reality (VR)

4e — Virtual Reality (VR)

▸ The team is exploring VR integration using Matterport technology and standard VR headset platforms. Matterport was used to capture a full digital 3D tour of the Unearthing the Legacy of Islamic Spain exhibition gallery at the Meadows Museum, allowing users to virtually walk through the space and examine the capital in its display context. All Matterport content and edits were produced by Anna Shovlin.
▸ Potential experience: placing the capital back in a virtual reconstruction of the Salón Rico at Madinat al-Zahrāʾ — experiencing it in architectural context.
▸ Status: VR integration is in planning stages — update this section as the project develops.

Dispersed Capitals

Purpose

This is the most ambitious and forward-looking section of the publication. It establishes the Quire publication as the foundation for a growing digital database of dispersed Madinat al-Zahrāʾ capitals held in collections worldwide. Each capital in the database gets its own catalogue entry page. This section will grow as the project expands.

Overview Essay Content
▸ Why are the capitals dispersed? After the sack of 1009–1010, the site was quarried for centuries — capitals were reused in churches, palaces, and private collections across Spain, North Africa, and Europe, or acquired by museums in the 19th–20th centuries.
▸ Current state of scholarship: Bugella Altamirano (2018) on Cordoban capitals in the Museo Arqueológico Nacional; Cressier (c.2007) on caliphal workshops and provincial distribution of capitals.
▸ The database mission: to reunite these dispersed fragments digitally — enabling comparative study of dimensions, carving style, workshop attribution, and inscription content across collections.
▸ How to contribute: invite scholars and institutions to share data and images — include a contact or submission mechanism (link to a Google Form or email address).

Excerpt from Walker Evans: Catalogue of the Collection (1995) by Judith Keller. Available for free download in its entirety, in the Getty Publications Virtual Library.

When Evans was officially hired in October 1935 as an Information Specialist by the Historical Section of the Resettlement Administration, his duties were described as follows: “Under the general supervision of the Chief of the Historical Section with wide latitude for the exercise of independent judgement and decision as Senior Information Specialist to carry out special assignments in the field; collect, compile and create photographic material to illustrate factual and interpretive news releases and other informational material upon all problems, progress and activities of the Resettlement Administration.”1 Evans was to make liberal use of his right to exercise “independent judgement” during his time with the RA, and he perpetually resisted the idea that his purpose there was to gather illustrations for the promotion of the RA’s (that is, the federal government’s New Deal) programs. While considering a position with the RA in the spring of 1935, he jotted down those things he would require of his employer, including the “guarantee of one-man performance,” and what he would provide, adding that he should not be asked to do anything more in the way of political propaganda: “[I] Mean never [to] make photographic statements for the government or do photographic chores for gov or anyone in gov, no matter how powerful—this is pure record not propaganda. The value and, if you like, even the propaganda value for the government lies in the record itself which in the long run will prove an intelligent and farsighted thing to have done. NO POLITICS whatever.” ()

Documenting American Life

Because his pictures had been issued by the agency with policy-approved captions for the past three years, Evans felt the need in 1938 to distance himself from that establishment, as well as the world of commercial publishing, by prefacing American Photographs with this statement: “The responsibility for the selection of the pictures used in this book has rested with the author, and the choice has been determined by his opinion: therefore they are presented without sponsorship or connection with the policies, aesthetic or political, of any of the institutions, publications or government agencies for which some of the work has been done.”2 Stryker’s business was to provide informative images to the mass media, and he and Evans would always disagree about the most appropriate vehicle for the latter’s photographs, as well as the definition of “documentary.” But when the photography project of the RA began, the two men were able to agree on its primary subject: American history as exemplified by life in the average American town. Evans’s vision for documenting American life had begun to form much earlier; a letter drafted to his friend Ernestine Evans, an editor at J. B. Lippincott, in February of 1934, makes clear his aspirations:

What do I want to do? … I know now is the time for picture books. An American city is the best, Pittsburgh better than Washington. I know more about such a place. I would want to visit several besides Pittsburgh before deciding. Something perhaps smaller. Toledo, Ohio, maybe. Then I’m not sure a book of photos should be identified locally. American city is what I’m after… People, all classes, surrounded by bunches of the new down-and-out. Automobiles and the automobile landscape. Architecture, American urban taste, commerce, small scale, large scale, the city street atmosphere, the street smell, the hateful stuff, women’s clubs, fake culture, bad education, religion in decay…3

The two men could find common ground in part due to the widespread influence of the 1929 publication Middletown: A Study in Modern American Culture by Robert and Helen Lynd, professors of sociology at Columbia and Sarah Lawrence, respectively. A 550-page field investigation by social anthropologists with subject headings including “What Middletown does to get its living” and “The houses in which Middletown lives,” this pioneering project attempted an objective analysis of life in a small Midwestern American city (Muncie, Indiana). The Lynds’ study was hailed as a very accessible report that was most appealing because it made “no attempt to prove anything” but simply recorded “what was observed.” The Lynds introduced their topic by saying their goal “was to study synchronously the interwoven trends that are the life of a small American city. A typical city, strictly speaking, does not exist, but the city studied was selected a shaving many features in common to a wide group of communities.” ()

The “Outline Memorandum” that Evans prepared in October 1935, probably at Stryker’s request, laying out plans for an eight-week automobile trip through the Southeast, makes reference to Middletown and presents thoughts similar to the photographer’s musings of 1934:

First objective, Pittsburgh and vicinity, one week; photography, documentary in style, of industrial subjects, emphasis on housing and home life of working-class people… Ohio Valley: rural architecture, including the historical, contemporary “Middletown” subjects; Cincinnati [sic] housing; notes on style of Victorian prosperous period… Indiana, Kentucky, Illinois river towns, gather typical documents, main streets, etc., in passing. Ditto Mississippi river towns. Select one of these towns, such as Hannibal, Missouri, for more thorough treatment, if time allows.4

This document goes on to list antebellum plantation architecture in Natchez, Mississippi; small rural French towns in Teche Parish, Louisiana; industrial themes in Birmingham, Alabama; and a cotton plantation in South Carolina, as objectives of the proposed trip for gathering still photography of a “general sociological nature.” In early 1936, one of Middletown’s authors had a chance to directly affect the RA’s Photography Section: Stryker showed Robert Lynd, a former Columbia colleague, some RA pictures and asked for his opinion while lunching with him in New York. The result was a “shooting script” for “things which should be photographed as American Background,” issued by Stryker to his team of photographers. The script contains an extensive listing of items like “People on and off the job,” “How do people look?,” “The wall decoration in homes as an index to the different income groups and their reactions,” and “A photographic study of use of leisure time in various income groups.”5

On Assignment

Once Evans was officially on staff, his first trip under Stryker’s direction seems to have been an extended fall visit to Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, where steel mills, workers’ housing, parading legionnaires, and elaborate gravestones (Figure 1, Figure 2, and Figure 3) were his subjects. Between November 1935 and April 1936, Evans made two lengthy road trips that would account for the bulk of his entire production for the RA and many of the 169 mid-thirties pictures that follow this essay. From November to mid-January, here turned to industrial centers in Pennsylvania, finally spending some time in Pittsburgh, and went on to Ohio, Kentucky, Tennessee, Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana, following to some extent the course outlined in the memorandum above. In February 1936, he left again, with a completely Southern itinerary that would take him through many small cities, some of them renowned for ante-bellum architecture and Civil War battles, in Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia, Florida, the Carolinas, and Virginia.

In the spring of 1936, Stryker approved a furlough for Evans to work on a Fortune story with James Agee. This leave for mid-July through mid-September allowed Evans to return to the “middle south” with Agee to prepare “an article on cotton tenantry in the United States, in the form of a photographic and verbal record of the daily lives and environment of an average white family of tenant farmers” (Figure 4 and Figure 5). () According to the terms of Stryker’s arrangement with Fortune’s art editor, the pictures Evans produced on this job would become the property of the RA after the magazine had run the finished essay in a fall issue.

Once Evans returned from this trip south, during which he and Agee documented the lives of the Burroughs, Fields, and Tengle families in Alabama (see Bud Fields with His Wife and Burrough’s Family), he spent September and October printing his pictures and preparing presentations for both Fortune and Stryker.6 Stryker again talked about a New England trip, on which he planned to accompany the photographer, but it did not materialize, and Evans was once more sent to the South, this time to photograph the catastrophe of flooding in Arkansas and Tennessee. An unusual and demanding assignment for Evans, this trip of late January and February 1937 was made with another RA photographer, Edwin Locke, and required that he spend considerable time in the affected area, photographing the flood victims and their temporary shelters.

Conclusion

This would be his last travel for the RA, an agency that was absorbed into the Farm Security Administration at about this time. Evans’s contributions to the RA’s documentation of Depression-era America had essentially been obtained between the summer of 1935 and the spring of 1936, a period of less than a year. The Alabama pictures made while on leave to Fortune would become his best-known photographs and, ironically, those most closely identified with his work as a New Deal photographer.

A Riotous Energy

There are many canvases the subjects of which are more
pathologic than artistic, subjects only fit for the
confessional or the privacy of the clinic. But, apart
from these disagreeable episodes, the main note of the
Salon

His research focuses on medieval Islamic art and architecture across the Mediterranean, with an emphasis on cross-cultural exchange, digital humanities, and innovative approaches to teaching and public scholarship.
    • Dallin Evans
      Dallin Evans is a PhD candidate at Rice University specializing in Caliphal architecture in tenth-century Islamic Spain. Trained as a practicing architect, he uses his expertise to analyze his areas of research. While earning his master’s in architecture, he studied Renaissance and Classical art and architecture at Syracuse University’s Florence campus, where he also curated several exhibitions. More recently, he has collaborated with Glair Anderson in the DLIVCC by creating digital models of archaeological findings at al-Rummaniyya, a villa near Cordoba Spain. Additionally, he has created drawings in publications for his advisors Farshid Emami and Sophie Crawford-Brown. As the 2024-25 Camfield Fellow at the Museum of Fine Art Houston, he created digital animations to accompany objects in the museum’s Islamic galleries. He recently co-organized a graduate conference on materiality at Rice, and a yearlong series of programs for “Archeology Now” on al-Andalus. He has presented his work at conferences across North America and Europe, supported by departmental and external awards.
      • Dr. Anna Ficek
        Agnieszka Anna Ficek is a Postdoctoral Fellow at the Meadows Museum’s Custard Institute for Spanish Art and Culture at Southern Methodist University and a Affiliated Researcher at the Edith O’Donnell Institute of Art History at the University of Texas at Dallas. She received her PhD in Art History from the City University of New York’s Graduate Center (2023). She is currently working on her first book project, From Allegory to Revolution: Latin America in the Early Modern French Imagination. Her research has been supported by grants, fellowships, and prizes from the Decorative Arts Trust, the Social Sciences Research Council, the German Center for Art History in Paris, the John Carter Brown Library, the Houghton Library at Harvard University, and the Huntington Library.
        • Dr. Luis Rueda Galan
          Luis Rueda Galán is the Mellon Postdoctoral Fellow at the Meadows Museum, Southern Methodist University. He obtained his Ph.D. in Art History (2021) from the École Pratique des Hautes Études-PSL in Paris (France) and the University of Jaén (Spain) with a dissertation on the conversion of mosques in medieval Iberia. His scholarly interests concentrate on transcultural and interreligious phenomena in the arts, particularly the legacy of al-Andalus in the rest of the Mediterranean and the image of Jerusalem and ideal reconstructions of the Temple of Solomon. He is also an expert on the influence of Leonardo da Vinci in early modern Spanish art. Rueda has been Juan de la Cierva postdoctoral fellow at the Institute of History in Madrid-CSIC, and visiting fellow at the Houghton Library-Harvard University, the French Institute for Oriental Archaeology in Cairo-IFAO, the Max Planck Institute for Art History in Florence-KHI, the School of Architecture-University of Florence and the Pontifical Gregorian University in Rome. Rueda has received a number of international research grants and distinctions, including the 2024 Gridley McKim-Smith Award from the Society for Iberian Global Art (SIGA) for an exceptional article by an early-career scholar.
          • Nida Jaffer
            Nida Jaffer studies the art and architecture of the medieval Islamic and Mediterranean worlds. Her current research centers on the eco-phenomenology of Islamic art and the materialization of the human-nature relationship in art and architecture. Nida earned her BA in History and MA in Art History from the University of Texas at Dallas at the Edith O’Donnell Institute of Art History. In parallel with her academic work, she has been working on digital humanities initiatives such as “The Muqaddimah,” an educational video game focused on the Great Mosque of Kairouan, and a documentary on medieval Fez, which integrates traditional filmmaking with digital animation. She is a founding member of the MENARAH initiative, a digital research hub dedicated to Islamic art and architecture, which integrates 3D modeling, game design, and documentary filmmaking into the study and preservation of material culture.

              • Anna Shovlin
                Anna Marisol Shovlin is a graduate student at the Edith O’Donnell Institute where she is pursuing an MA in Art History. Her research explores ancient and early medieval domestic art and architecture in Italy and Spain. She holds a BA from the University of Texas at Dallas in Arts, Technology, and Emerging Communication with a concentration in 3D Animation and Video Game Design. She now works as an archaeologist and art historian combining traditional archaeological methodologies with innovative digital advancements such as 3D modeling, LiDar and photogrammetry scanning, and aerial mapping. Her field work includes research with The Oplontis Project on their archaeological sites near Pompeii. With a passion for digital cultural heritage and preservation, her work aims to utilize a multidisciplinary approach to art historical research.
