From the beginning, the collaborative's ambition was not only to see the capital differently but to make it touchable. Working from the STL files generated by the scan, the team has produced the capital in multiple printed versions, each serving a different purpose in the galleries and the classroom.

The first prototypes were produced at SMU's Deason Innovation Gym (DIG) and UT Dallas's 3D printing lab using fused deposition modeling (FDM) printers and 1.75mm ABS filament — the workhorse technology of consumer 3D printing, in which molten plastic is extruded layer by layer. Because the capital exceeds the build volume of these printers, it was printed in five interlocking segments, joined with Velcro so the model can be assembled and taken apart with ease. This modularity has proven to be a feature rather than a workaround: in teaching settings, disassembling the capital lets students examine its internal logic and handle individual sections up close.

A second generation of higher-fidelity reproductions followed, produced in collaboration with Dr. Matthew Boulanger of SMU's Archaeological Research Collections (ARC) using a 3D Systems ZPrinter 450. Unlike filament printing, the ZPrinter builds forms from a gypsum-based composite powder, bound layer by layer with a starch-based inkjet binder. The freshly printed pieces emerge fragile and chalky; treated with ZBond, a cyanoacrylate infiltrant, they gain structural strength and a smooth, slightly glossy surface that comes strikingly close to the look and feel of marble. The result is a reproduction that communicates not just the capital's geometry but something of its material presence.

These models have transformed how the capital meets its public. In the Meadows galleries, visitors can hold the printed capital while standing before the original — running their fingers over the deep undercuts of the acanthus carving and the ridges of the Kufic inscription that glass and distance would otherwise keep out of reach. For visitors with visual impairments, the replica offers something the vitrine cannot: direct, tactile access to the object's form, an accessibility dimension the team considers central to the project rather than incidental to it. The prints have anchored high school class visits and hands-on art-making workshops, and both the replicas and the digital model are being integrated into art history and archaeology courses at SMU and UT Dallas, where they support close looking, discussions of medieval carving technique, and conversations about what digital reproduction can — and cannot — convey about an original work of art.
