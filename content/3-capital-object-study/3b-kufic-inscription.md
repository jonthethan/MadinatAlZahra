Wrapped around the abacus of the Meadows capital, a band of angular Kufic script carries the object's most direct message from the tenth century: a blessing that begins at one corner, unfolds face by face around all four sides, and returns to its starting point — a text designed, like the capital itself, to be read in the round. Dr. Ali Asgar H. Alibhai, Assistant Professor of Islamic Art History and Material Culture at the University of Texas at Dallas, has produced the first complete reading and translation of the inscription, presented here as a sequential walkthrough of its nine segments. His analysis not only recovers the text but reveals its source — a Qurʾanic verse whose imagery of paradise speaks directly to the capital's origins at Madinat al-Zahrāʾ.

### Reading the Inscription: A Walkthrough in Nine Segments

The inscription begins at a corner of the abacus and proceeds around the capital, with phrases frequently running across the boundaries between faces — so that the text can only be fully understood when the segments are read in continuous sequence.

**Segment 1.** The inscription opens at the corner with bism (بسم), "In the name of…" — the first word of the basmala, the invocation that opens nearly every chapter of the Qurʾan.

**Segment 2.** Continuing onto the adjacent side, Allāh al-Raḥīm (الرحيم هللا), "God, the Merciful." Read together with Segment 1, the opening face declares: "In the name of God, the Merciful."

**Segment 3.** The next phrase reads tabāraka alladhī (الذي تبارك), "Blessed is He who…" The final letter yāʾ is no longer legible, likely lost to damage on the capital, but the reading is secure from context.

**Segment 4.** At the following face, the corner has suffered damage and the first word survives only partially: a shīn and part of an alif, reconstructed as shāʾa, "willed." It is followed by the fully legible jaʿala, "would make."

**Segment 5.** The face concludes with laka khayr (خير لك), "for you benevolence." Read together, Segments 3 through 5 yield tabāraka alladhī ʾin shāʾa jaʿala laka khayr — and here the inscription reveals its source. This is the opening of Qurʾan 25:10, from Sūrat al-Furqān, the verse that continues: "Blessed is He who, if He willed, could have made for you [something] better than that — gardens beneath which rivers flow — and could make for you palaces." (In the capital's rendering, the final alif of khayran is absent.)

**Segment 6.** The next face opens with Allāh baraka (بركة هللا). The word Allāh completes the preceding Qurʾanic phrase — "God's benevolence" — while baraka, "blessing," begins a new phrase that continues onto the following segment.

**Segment 7.** Min Allāh (هللا من), completing the phrase baraka min Allāh, "Blessings from God" — a common ornamental blessing formula on objects from the medieval Islamic world.

**Segment 8.** Ghibṭa (غبطة), "happiness" or "bliss."

**Segment 9.** Kāmila (كاملة), "complete." Read with Segment 8, the inscription closes with ghibṭa kāmila, "complete happiness," arriving back at the corner where bism began — the text forming an unbroken ring of blessing around the capital.

### Why This Verse Matters:

The citation of Qurʾan 25:10 is the inscription's most significant feature. The full verse promises gardens beneath which rivers flow, and palaces — imagery that maps with striking precision onto Madinat al-Zahrāʾ itself, with its terraced gardens, water features, and royal residences. As Dr. Alibhai argues, the choice of this verse strengthens the capital's attribution to the caliphal city and suggests it once crowned a column in one of the royal buildings of the palace complex. Detached from its full Qurʾanic context, the phrasing functions as a blessing of benevolence and goodwill — but for anyone who knew the verse, the allusion to paradise, and to the paradisiacal city around them, would have been unmistakable.

The closing formulas belong to a broader visual culture of blessing. Baraka min Allāh and ghibṭa kāmila are well-attested ornamental phrases across the medieval Islamic Mediterranean, appearing frequently in the luxury arts of al-Andalus — on carved ivories, including pyxides and caskets, and on metalwork. Their presence here places the capital within a shared epigraphic vocabulary that linked architecture to the portable arts of the Umayyad court.

### A Note on Condition:

Portions of the inscription are no longer visible, likely destroyed by damage to the capital over its long history — including the final yāʾ of alladhī (Segment 3), the opening of shāʾa (Segment 4), and the final alif of khayran (Segment 5). Dr. Alibhai's report identifies each reconstructed passage; the readings are supported by the surviving letter forms, the grammar of the surrounding text, and the Qurʾanic source. The 3D scan presented in this publication preserves the inscription's current state in high resolution, providing a permanent record against future loss.
