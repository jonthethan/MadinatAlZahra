**Unknown maker**  
*Capital from Madinat al-Zahrāʾ*, c. 965  
Carved marble  
Meadows Museum, SMU, Dallas. Museum purchase, Meadows Museum Acquisition Fund, MM.96.01  

At the heart of this publication is the object itself. This ornately carved marble capital originates from Madinat al-Zahrāʾ, the palace-city constructed for the Umayyad caliph ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III (r. 912–961) as the seat of his government on high ground just outside Córdoba. Carved by a local workshop around 965, it exemplifies the classic caliphal type: a Corinthian-derived structure of drum, corner volutes, and flaring echinus, enveloped in deeply undercut acanthus leaves and interlaced vegetal and geometric ornament, with a string of gem-shaped motifs running along the upper portion of the drum. On the abacus, a band of angular Kufic script carries phrases of blessing drawn from the Qurʾan — an inscription examined in full below.

### The 3D Model

{% figure 'rotating-capital' %}

The rotating model presented here is not a film of the capital but a rendering of its digital twin. In 2025, the collaborative scanned the capital using an EinScan HX2, a handheld scanner that combines structured-light and laser scanning to capture fine surface detail. Passing the scanner over the object from every angle, the team recorded millions of measurement points, which were then aligned and processed into a high-resolution polygon mesh — a complete three-dimensional record of the capital's geometry, from the deepest undercuts of the acanthus carving to the shallow ridges of the inscribed letters. The rotation you see was rendered from that scan data by Anna Shovlin, the project's Technology Lead and 3D Imaging Specialist (UT Dallas), and allows the capital to be examined in the round — a view no gallery installation can offer, revealing faces of the object normally turned away from the visitor and making legible the continuous path of the inscription as it wraps around all four sides of the abacus.

The digital model is more than an illustration: it is a research instrument. Raking virtual light across the mesh brings out tool marks and carving sequences invisible to the naked eye, and the scan preserves the capital's current condition — including areas of damage and loss — as a benchmark for future conservation. It also underpins the project's tactile reproductions and the open questions explored elsewhere in this publication, including whether the capital may have been recarved from an earlier Roman Corinthian capital.

### The Inscription

Running along the abacus is a Kufic inscription that begins at one corner and unfolds face by face around the capital. Dr. Ali Asgar H. Alibhai's complete reading, presented here, traces the text through each of its nine segments: an invocation, a partial citation of Qurʾan 25:10 (Sūrat al-Furqān) — the verse promising gardens beneath which rivers flow, and palaces — and the ornamental blessing phrases baraka min Allāh and ghibṭa kāmila, “blessings from God” and “complete happiness.” The choice of this particular verse is itself evidence: its paradisiacal imagery of gardens, waters, and palaces echoes the splendor of Madinat al-Zahrāʾ, suggesting the capital once stood in one of the royal buildings of the palace complex.
