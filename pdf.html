Digitizing al-Andalus : The Madinat al-Zahrāʾ Capital and the Digital Study of Dispersed Umayyad Architecture

Madinat al-Zahrāʾ 3D Capital Digitization and Research Collaborative

Contributors: Cristina Aldrich, Dr. Ali Asgar Alibhai, Dallin Evans, Dr. Anna Ficek, Dr. Luis Rueda Galan, Nida Jaffer, Jonathan Lee, Anna Shovlin, and Ciaran Vaille

Digitizing al-Andalus explores what digital technologies can reveal about a masterwork of medieval Islamic art: a tenth-century marble capital from Madinat al-Zahrāʾ, the palatial city of the Umayyad caliphate just outside Córdoba, now in the collection of the Meadows Museum. Carved during the reign of the caliph ʿAbd al-Rahman III (r. 912–961), the capital features deeply cut acanthus leaves, interlaced vegetal motifs, and Arabic inscriptions on its abacus, and is one of countless architectural fragments dispersed from the site across collections worldwide.

The Madinat al-Zahrāʾ 3D Capital Digitization and Research Collaborative, formed by researchers at the Meadows Museum, the University of Texas at Dallas, and Southern Methodist University, brings together art historians, engineers, archaeologists, and digital specialists to document, analyze, and activate this object through new means: high-resolution 3D scanning, tactile full-scale reproduction, virtual exhibition, and collaborative scholarship. This publication traces the project’s story, from the capital’s historical context and inscriptions to the digitization process and its outcomes, while situating the object within the broader phenomenon of Umayyad spolia and their afterlives. Conceived as a living publication, the site will continue to grow as contributors add new research, essays, and digital resources, reflecting our conviction that the study of dispersed heritage is, by nature, an ongoing and collective endeavor.

I. Introduction: About the Project

A single marble capital, carved in tenth-century Córdoba, holds within its stone the ambitions of a dynasty.

بسم هللا الرحيم تبارك الذي إن شاء جعل لك خير هللا. بركة من للا . .غبط ككامل

Bism Allāh al-Rahim, tabāraka alladhī ʾin shāʾa jaʿala laka khayr Allāh. Baraka min Allāh. Ghibṭa kāmila.

In the name of Allāh the Merciful. Blessed is He who, if he willed, would make for you (bestow upon you) Allāh's benevolence. Blessings from Allāh. Complete Happiness.

— Reading and translation by Dr. Ali Asgar H. Alibhai

Deeply carved acanthus leaves unfurl across its drum; four volutes anchor its corners; a band of gem-like forms encircles its upper edge. And along the abacus, in angular Kufic script, runs a blessing drawn from the Qurʾan — an invocation of the God who bestows palaces and gardens with rivers flowing beneath. Carved around 965 by a workshop serving the Umayyad caliphs of al-Andalus, this marble capital once crowned a column at Madinat al-Zahrāʾ, the “most radiant city,” the palatine capital built by ʿAbd al-Rahman III (r. 912–961) on the slopes above Córdoba. The city stood for less than a century before civil war reduced it to fragments; the capital, now in the collection of the Meadows Museum (MM.96.01), is one of the dispersed pieces through which that lost splendor can still be touched (literally), as this project has sought to make possible.

Exhibition Origin

This project grew out of research for the exhibition Unearthing the Legacy of Islamic Spain (Meadows Museum, September 15, 2024–January 5, 2025), which brought together nineteenth- and early twentieth-century prints, drawings, paintings, and books to examine how Spain’s Islamic artistic heritage was imagined, romanticized, and contested. At the heart of the exhibition stood the capital itself — the earliest object on view and a material anchor for the questions the show posed about memory, legacy, and the afterlives of al-Andalus. In preparing the exhibition, we began to ask how digital technology might deepen both scholarly understanding and public engagement with an object like this one. That question outlived the exhibition and became this collaborative.

Project Mission Statement

The Madinat al-Zahrāʾ 3D Capital Digitization and Research Collaborative brings together art historians, archaeologists, engineers, and students to document, analyze, and activate this object through digital means. Its goals are threefold: digital preservation, through the creation of a high-resolution 3D model that records the capital’s geometry, tool marks, and inscriptions for future study; scholarly analysis, including new epigraphic readings, provenance research, and investigation of the capital’s materiality — among them the question of whether it may have been recarved from an earlier Roman Corinthian capital; and public engagement, through tactile reproductions, virtual tours, and this evolving digital publication, conceived as a living resource that will grow as research continues.

What We Have Done So Far

Over the past two years the collaborative has moved from experiment to established practice. Working with JT Ringer, Lab Manager of SMU’s Deason Innovation Gym (DIG), and Anna Shovlin, the project’s Technology Lead at UT Dallas, the team scanned the capital using an EinScan HX2 handheld scanner — a hybrid structured-light and laser system that captured the object from multiple angles to produce a high-resolution polygon mesh. From that model, DIG fabricated a full-scale plastic reproduction printed in five interlocking sections, designed with accessibility in mind so that visitors — including those with visual impairments — can explore the capital’s intricate geometry through touch. A smaller-scale version, printed by Professor Matthew Boulanger of SMU’s Archaeological Research Collections using a ZPrinter 450, serves as a portable teaching tool.

These reproductions have already transformed how the capital is taught. In gallery programs led with the Meadows Museum’s Education Department, students stand before the original while holding its printed double in their hands — feeling the depth of the acanthus carving and the ridges of the Kufic letters that museum glass and distance would otherwise keep out of reach. The models have supported high school visits, hands-on art-making workshops, and university teaching, demonstrating the power of multisensory learning across audiences.

In November 2025, the project reached an international scholarly audience at the Middle East Studies Association (MESA) annual conference in Washington, DC, where Cristina Aldrich and Dallin Evans both presented as part of the panel Digital Reimaginings of Middle Eastern Studies: Constructing the Medieval Mediterranean in the Age of the Great Caliphs, organized by Professor Ali Asgar Alibhai and sponsored by Middle East Medievalists.

Team and Institutional Partners

The collaborative links four institutional partners: the Meadows Museum, SMU, home of the capital and lead institution; the Custard Institute for Spanish Art and Culture, SMU, which facilitates the project and its publication; the Edith O’Donnell Institute of Art History at the University of Texas at Dallas, whose faculty and students lead the project’s research and imaging work, with support from the MENARAH Initiative; and the Deason Innovation Gym (DIG) at SMU’s Lyle School of Engineering, where the scanning was tested and the 3D printing realized. The project also benefits from growing international partnerships, including with the Instituto de Historia, CSIC, in Madrid.

Publication Structure

This publication is designed to be explored rather than simply read. The Capital and Its Inscriptions presents the object itself, with high-resolution imagery and Dr. Alibhai’s complete reading of its Kufic text. The 3D Model invites you to rotate, zoom, and examine the capital in the round, as no vitrine allows. The Virtual Exhibition returns the capital to its gallery context through an immersive Matterport tour. Process: Scanning and Printing takes you behind the scenes of the digitization, from first scanner tests at DIG to the assembly of the full-scale print. Research Essays gather contributions from the collaborative’s members — on epigraphy, provenance, the dispersal and afterlife of Madinat al-Zahrāʾ’s marbles, and 3D methodology — and will continue to grow as new work is completed. Like the city whose fragments it studies, this publication is not a finished monument but a site of ongoing excavation: we invite you to return as it evolves.

The Umayyad Caliphate in al-Andalus

2a — The Umayyad Caliphate in al-Andalus

▸ Background on the Umayyad dynasty’s arrival in Iberia (711 CE) and the establishment of the emirate, then caliphate, at Córdoba.
▸ ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III (r. 912–961): proclamation of the caliphate in 929; Córdoba as the most sophisticated city in western Europe.
▸ Competition with the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad and the Fatimid caliphate in North Africa — architecture as political rhetoric.
▸ Key source: Calvo Capilla, S. (2018), ‘The Visual Construction of the Umayyad Caliphate in Al-Andalus through the Great Mosque of Cordoba.’

Madinat al-Zahra - The Brilliant City

2b — Madinat al-Zahrāʾ - The Brilliant City

▸ Founded 936 CE on the slopes of the Sierra Morena above Córdoba — three terraces of palaces, mosques, gardens.
▸ Name meaning: ‘most radiant city’ or ‘shining city’ — architectural manifesto of caliphal legitimacy.
▸ Key spaces: the Salón Rico (Rich Hall / Throne Hall of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III), administrative quarters, mosque, gardens.
▸ Destruction: the fitna of 1009–1010 — civil war, sacking, and the city’s disappearance under farmland for ~1,000 years.
▸ Today: UNESCO World Heritage Site; active archaeological excavation; on-site museum.
▸ Key source: Vallejo Triano, A. (2015), ‘Madinat al-Zahrāʾ: Historical Reality and Present-Day Heritage.’

The Architecture of Capitals - Rhetoric in Stone

2c — The Architecture of Capitals - Rhetoric in Stone

▸ The Corinthian capital as a Mediterranean form reactivated and transformed by Umayyad craftsmen.
▸ Caliphal capitals: dense acanthus foliage, corner volutes, interlaced vegetal and geometric motifs — distinct from Roman precedents.
▸ The reuse of Roman spolia: thousands of antique columns imported from Ifriqiya (Tunisia/Algeria) and Tripolitania (Libya) — hybrid materiality.
▸ The inscription as architectural element: Kufic blessings on the abacus — invocations of divine authority woven into structure.
▸ Scholarly debate: Cressier, P. (2010) on the role of the capital in caliphal architectural discourse; Cressier, P. (1995) on the Rich Hall capitals.s

Capital Object Study

At the heart of this publication is the object itself. This ornately carved marble capital originates from Madinat al-Zahrāʾ, the palace-city constructed for the Umayyad caliph ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III (r. 912–961) as the seat of his government on high ground just outside Córdoba. Carved by a local workshop around 965, it exemplifies the classic caliphal type: a Corinthian-derived structure of drum, corner volutes, and flaring echinus, enveloped in deeply undercut acanthus leaves and interlaced vegetal and geometric ornament, with a string of gem-shaped motifs running along the upper portion of the drum. On the abacus, a band of angular Kufic script carries phrases of blessing drawn from the Qurʾan — an inscription examined in full below.

The 3D Model

The rotating model presented here is not a film of the capital but a rendering of its digital twin. In 2025, the collaborative scanned the capital using an EinScan HX2, a handheld scanner that combines structured-light and laser scanning to capture fine surface detail. Passing the scanner over the object from every angle, the team recorded millions of measurement points, which were then aligned and processed into a high-resolution polygon mesh — a complete three-dimensional record of the capital’s geometry, from the deepest undercuts of the acanthus carving to the shallow ridges of the inscribed letters. The rotation you see was rendered from that scan data by Anna Shovlin, the project’s Technology Lead and 3D Imaging Specialist (UT Dallas), and allows the capital to be examined in the round — a view no gallery installation can offer, revealing faces of the object normally turned away from the visitor and making legible the continuous path of the inscription as it wraps around all four sides of the abacus.

The digital model is more than an illustration: it is a research instrument. Raking virtual light across the mesh brings out tool marks and carving sequences invisible to the naked eye, and the scan preserves the capital’s current condition — including areas of damage and loss — as a benchmark for future conservation. It also underpins the project’s tactile reproductions and the open questions explored elsewhere in this publication, including whether the capital may have been recarved from an earlier Roman Corinthian capital.

The Inscription

Running along the abacus is a Kufic inscription that begins at one corner and unfolds face by face around the capital. Dr. Ali Asgar H. Alibhai’s complete reading, presented here, traces the text through each of its nine segments: an invocation, a partial citation of Qurʾan 25:10 (Sūrat al-Furqān) — the verse promising gardens beneath which rivers flow, and palaces — and the ornamental blessing phrases baraka min Allāh and ghibṭa kāmila, “blessings from God” and “complete happiness.” The choice of this particular verse is itself evidence: its paradisiacal imagery of gardens, waters, and palaces echoes the splendor of Madinat al-Zahrāʾ, suggesting the capital once stood in one of the royal buildings of the palace complex.

The Kufic Inscription

Wrapped around the abacus of the Meadows capital, a band of angular Kufic script carries the object’s most direct message from the tenth century: a blessing that begins at one corner, unfolds face by face around all four sides, and returns to its starting point — a text designed, like the capital itself, to be read in the round. Dr. Ali Asgar H. Alibhai, Assistant Professor of Islamic Art History and Material Culture at the University of Texas at Dallas, has produced the first complete reading and translation of the inscription, presented here as a sequential walkthrough of its nine segments. His analysis not only recovers the text but reveals its source — a Qurʾanic verse whose imagery of paradise speaks directly to the capital’s origins at Madinat al-Zahrāʾ.

بسم هللا الرحيم تبارك الذي إن شاء جعل لك خير هللا. بركة من للا . .غبط ككامل

Bism Allāh al-Raḥīm, tabāraka alladhī ʾin shāʾa jaʿala laka khayr Allāh. Baraka min Allāh. Ghibṭa kāmila.

In the name of God the Merciful. Blessed is He who, if he willed, would make for you God's benevolence. Blessings from God. Complete Happiness.

— Reading and translation by Ali Asgar H. Alibhai

Reading the Inscription: A Walkthrough in Nine Segments

The inscription begins at a corner of the abacus and proceeds around the capital, with phrases frequently running across the boundaries between faces — so that the text can only be fully understood when the segments are read in continuous sequence.

Segment 1. The inscription opens at the corner with bism (بسم), “In the name of…” — the first word of the basmala, the invocation that opens nearly every chapter of the Qurʾan.

Segment 2. Continuing onto the adjacent side, Allāh al-Raḥīm (الرحيم هللا), “God, the Merciful.” Read together with Segment 1, the opening face declares: “In the name of God, the Merciful.”

Segment 3. The next phrase reads tabāraka alladhī (الذي تبارك), “Blessed is He who…” The final letter yāʾ is no longer legible, likely lost to damage on the capital, but the reading is secure from context.

Segment 4. At the following face, the corner has suffered damage and the first word survives only partially: a shīn and part of an alif, reconstructed as shāʾa, “willed.” It is followed by the fully legible jaʿala, “would make.”

Segment 5. The face concludes with laka khayr (خير لك), “for you benevolence.” Read together, Segments 3 through 5 yield tabāraka alladhī ʾin shāʾa jaʿala laka khayr — and here the inscription reveals its source. This is the opening of Qurʾan 25:10, from Sūrat al-Furqān, the verse that continues: “Blessed is He who, if He willed, could have made for you [something] better than that — gardens beneath which rivers flow — and could make for you palaces.” (In the capital’s rendering, the final alif of khayran is absent.)

Segment 6. The next face opens with Allāh baraka (بركة هللا). The word Allāh completes the preceding Qurʾanic phrase — “God’s benevolence” — while baraka, “blessing,” begins a new phrase that continues onto the following segment.

Segment 7. Min Allāh (هللا من), completing the phrase baraka min Allāh, “Blessings from God” — a common ornamental blessing formula on objects from the medieval Islamic world.

Segment 8. Ghibṭa (غبطة), “happiness” or “bliss.”

Segment 9. Kāmila (كاملة), “complete.” Read with Segment 8, the inscription closes with ghibṭa kāmila, “complete happiness,” arriving back at the corner where bism began — the text forming an unbroken ring of blessing around the capital.

Why This Verse Matters:

The citation of Qurʾan 25:10 is the inscription’s most significant feature. The full verse promises gardens beneath which rivers flow, and palaces — imagery that maps with striking precision onto Madinat al-Zahrāʾ itself, with its terraced gardens, water features, and royal residences. As Dr. Alibhai argues, the choice of this verse strengthens the capital’s attribution to the caliphal city and suggests it once crowned a column in one of the royal buildings of the palace complex. Detached from its full Qurʾanic context, the phrasing functions as a blessing of benevolence and goodwill — but for anyone who knew the verse, the allusion to paradise, and to the paradisiacal city around them, would have been unmistakable.

The closing formulas belong to a broader visual culture of blessing. Baraka min Allāh and ghibṭa kāmila are well-attested ornamental phrases across the medieval Islamic Mediterranean, appearing frequently in the luxury arts of al-Andalus — on carved ivories, including pyxides and caskets, and on metalwork. Their presence here places the capital within a shared epigraphic vocabulary that linked architecture to the portable arts of the Umayyad court.

A Note on Condition:

Portions of the inscription are no longer visible, likely destroyed by damage to the capital over its long history — including the final yāʾ of alladhī (Segment 3), the opening of shāʾa (Segment 4), and the final alif of khayran (Segment 5). Dr. Alibhai’s report identifies each reconstructed passage; the readings are supported by the surviving letter forms, the grammar of the surrounding text, and the Qurʾanic source. The 3D scan presented in this publication preserves the inscription’s current state in high resolution, providing a permanent record against future loss.

3c — Roman Spolia and the Question of Recarving

▸ Evidence for recarving: proportional analysis; structural anomalies visible in 3D scan; comparison with known reused Roman capitals at Madinat al-Zahrāʾ.

3D Scanning

Digitizing the capital began with light. In sessions held in the Meadows galleries and at SMU’s Deason Innovation Gym (DIG), the team scanned the object using an EinScan HX2, a handheld scanner that combines structured-light and laser scanning to capture fine surface detail without ever touching the marble. Anna Shovlin, the project’s 3D Imaging Specialist (UT Dallas), passed the scanner slowly over the capital from every angle — its deeply undercut acanthus leaves and shallow inscribed letters posing very different challenges for the light — while the software aligned millions of measurement points in real time into a single high-resolution polygon mesh.

Early sessions with DIG Lab Manager JT Ringer were devoted to testing the scanner and troubleshooting the capital’s trickiest surfaces; later ones refined the model into STL and OBJ files that now serve as the foundation for everything else in this project, from the rotating model and 3D prints to research on tool marks and carving technique. The photographs below document that process.

Why Digital Methods?

4a — Why Digital Methods?

▸ Traditional photography cannot capture the full three-dimensionality of a carved capital — a 3D model allows 360° examination of carving depth, undercutting, and surface texture.
▸ The digital scan creates a permanent, non-contact record — a resource for conservation, future scholars, and institutions without physical access to the object.
▸ Foundation for a larger database of dispersed Madinat al-Zahrāʾ capitals worldwide (see Section 5).

What the Scan Reveals

4c — What the Scan Reveals

▸ Tool marks and carving sequence: the 3D model makes visible the order in which different elements were carved — offering clues to workshop practice.
▸ Structural evidence for the recarving hypothesis: the model allows measurement and comparison of proportional systems that suggest a Roman Corinthian prototype.
▸ Inscription legibility: the scan captures inscription segments that are difficult to read with the naked eye under flat lighting — raking light views generated from the 3D model enhance legibility. In addition, Dr. Luis Rueda Galán (Meadows Museum, SMU) conducted a hands-on examination of the capital using UV light, which revealed surface details and possible traces of material treatments not visible under standard lighting conditions. UV examination results should be cross-referenced with the 3D model in the final publication.

3D Printing and Public Engagement

From the beginning, the collaborative’s ambition was not only to see the capital differently but to make it touchable. Working from the STL files generated by the scan, the team has produced the capital in multiple printed versions, each serving a different purpose in the galleries and the classroom.

The first prototypes were produced at SMU’s Deason Innovation Gym (DIG) and UT Dallas’s 3D printing lab using fused deposition modeling (FDM) printers and 1.75mm ABS filament — the workhorse technology of consumer 3D printing, in which molten plastic is extruded layer by layer. Because the capital exceeds the build volume of these printers, it was printed in five interlocking segments, joined with Velcro so the model can be assembled and taken apart with ease. This modularity has proven to be a feature rather than a workaround: in teaching settings, disassembling the capital lets students examine its internal logic and handle individual sections up close.

A second generation of higher-fidelity reproductions followed, produced in collaboration with Dr. Matthew Boulanger of SMU’s Archaeological Research Collections (ARC) using a 3D Systems ZPrinter 450. Unlike filament printing, the ZPrinter builds forms from a gypsum-based composite powder, bound layer by layer with a starch-based inkjet binder. The freshly printed pieces emerge fragile and chalky; treated with ZBond, a cyanoacrylate infiltrant, they gain structural strength and a smooth, slightly glossy surface that comes strikingly close to the look and feel of marble. The result is a reproduction that communicates not just the capital’s geometry but something of its material presence.

These models have transformed how the capital meets its public. In the Meadows galleries, visitors can hold the printed capital while standing before the original — running their fingers over the deep undercuts of the acanthus carving and the ridges of the Kufic inscription that glass and distance would otherwise keep out of reach. For visitors with visual impairments, the replica offers something the vitrine cannot: direct, tactile access to the object’s form, an accessibility dimension the team considers central to the project rather than incidental to it. The prints have anchored high school class visits and hands-on art-making workshops, and both the replicas and the digital model are being integrated into art history and archaeology courses at SMU and UT Dallas, where they support close looking, discussions of medieval carving technique, and conversations about what digital reproduction can — and cannot — convey about an original work of art.

Virtual Reality: Walking Through the Exhibition

Although Unearthing the Legacy of Islamic Spain closed its doors in January 2025, the exhibition remains open here — permanently, and to anyone in the world. Using Matterport, a 3D capture technology that combines panoramic photography with spatial depth mapping, the team recorded a complete digital twin of the exhibition gallery at the Meadows Museum. Matterport works by scanning a space from dozens of positions and stitching those captures into a seamless, navigable three-dimensional model: the result is not a video or a slideshow but a virtual environment you can move through at your own pace, as if walking the gallery floor yourself.

In the embedded tour below, visitors can navigate freely through the exhibition — moving from room to room, turning to take in the installation from any angle, and approaching the capital to examine it in its display context, surrounded by the nineteenth-century prints, drawings, paintings, and books with which it was originally in dialogue. Interactive tags embedded throughout the space invite deeper exploration: clicking on objects and wall labels opens additional information about each work, so that the tour functions not merely as a record of the exhibition but as an annotated, self-guided visit. For researchers, the capture also preserves something exhibitions rarely leave behind — a complete spatial document of curatorial choices, sightlines, and juxtapositions. All Matterport content and edits were produced by Anna Shovlin, the project’s Technology Lead and 3D Imaging Specialist (UT Dallas).

The Matterport tour is only the first step in the project’s virtual ambitions. The team is exploring further VR integration using standard headset platforms, with one experience in particular on the horizon: placing the capital back where it began. A virtual reconstruction of the Salón Rico — the sumptuous reception hall of Madinat al-Zahrāʾ — would allow visitors to encounter the capital not in a museum vitrine but in architectural context, atop a column amid the polychrome carving and processional spaces of the caliphal court. VR integration remains in the planning stages; this section will be updated as the work develops — one of the ways this publication, like the project itself, continues to grow.

Mapping Dispersed Capitals

Madinat al-Zahrāʾ survives today in fragments — and those fragments are everywhere. When civil war engulfed the caliphate in the fitna of 1009–1010, the palace-city was sacked and burned, and for centuries afterward its ruins served as a quarry[cite: 1]. Capitals, column shafts, and carved panels were carried off and given new lives: reused in churches and palaces across Spain, incorporated into buildings in North Africa, and eventually scattered through private collections and, in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, acquired by museums across Europe and the Americas[cite: 1]. The Meadows capital is one node in this vast diaspora of marble[cite: 1]. Every dispersed fragment carries a piece of the city’s history with it — evidence of workshop practice, carving technique, epigraphic programs, and the shifting tastes and markets that moved caliphal stone across a millennium and around the globe[cite: 1].

This section is the most ambitious and forward-looking part of the publication: the foundation for a growing digital database of dispersed Madinat al-Zahrāʾ capitals held in collections worldwide[cite: 1]. Its purpose is to reunite, digitally, what history has scattered[cite: 1]. By cataloguing capitals across collections — each with its own entry recording dimensions, carving style, condition, inscription content, and provenance — the database will enable the kind of comparative study that dispersal has long made difficult: tracing workshop attributions across borders, comparing epigraphic programs between fragments now separated by oceans, and mapping the routes by which caliphal marble traveled from Córdoba’s hinterland to the world’s museums[cite: 1]. In doing so, the project builds on a foundation of important scholarship, including Bugella Altamirano’s study (2018) of the Cordoban capitals in the Museo Arqueológico Nacional in Madrid and Cressier’s work on caliphal workshops and the provincial distribution of capitals, while extending that scholarship into a shared, cumulative digital resource[cite: 1].

Crucially, this is a living catalogue[cite: 1]. The entries published here represent the beginning, not the end, of the undertaking: the team is actively researching and adding fragments as they are identified and documented, and this section will continue to grow with the project[cite: 1]. We warmly invite scholars, curators, and institutions who hold — or know of — capitals and architectural fragments from Madinat al-Zahrāʾ to contribute data and images to the database[cite: 1]. To share information or propose an addition, please contact the project team at [email address / submission form link][cite: 1]. Every contribution helps restore a measure of coherence to one of the great dispersed monuments of the medieval Mediterranean[cite: 1].
